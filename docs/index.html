What you are looking at

That report is an audit of a quarterly accounts workbook we broke on purpose — fifteen invoices, a summary, a hidden rates sheet. It is the kind of file that exists in every small business.

Every fault in it looks completely normal on the sheet. That is the point. Nothing is red, nothing says #VALUE!, every individual formula reads correctly. Here is some of what came back:

WhereWhat
C9 high Someone typed 246.00 over the VAT formula. The column still looks like a column of formulas. That row stopped updating months ago.
B18 high The total sums B2:B14. Two rows were added underneath and it never grew to meet them.
B13 high The regional total sweeps a block that already contains a subtotal of part of it, so those rows are counted twice. Every formula involved is correct on its own.
B6 high The number saved in the cell is not what its formula computes. Calculation was switched off, a figure moved, and the file was sent anyway.
B17 high A link to last year's workbook, which is no longer where it was. The cell keeps showing the value it last saw.
B15 medium Five commission rates buried inside one formula, seven levels deep. Nobody is going to inherit that safely.

It tells you what it did not check

Every report carries a line like this — the number you would want most from anybody's auditor, and the one least often given: 

recomputed 37 of 41 formulas · 4 fell outside the evaluated subset

A tool that silently checks two-thirds of a workbook lets you believe it checked all of it. An unverified formula has not been checked. It is not a formula that passed.

Tickmark recomputes a small, closed set — arithmetic plus SUM, AVERAGE, MIN, MAX, COUNT, COUNTA, ROUND, ROUNDUP, ROUNDDOWN and ABS — and refuses everything else by name rather than guessing at it. Then it tells you how much that covered.

Why it reports what it didn't check

That coverage line is the most deliberate decision in the tool, and the argument behind it is written up separately: Your spreadsheet auditor should tell you what it didn't check — on why full formula evaluation is a trap, and what refusing properly has to mean in practice.

Where it runs

  • Entirely on your machine. No model, no network, no API key, no account. Nothing is uploaded.
  • It never writes to your workbooks. Reports only, no auto-fixing.
  • Reads legacy .xls natively — the Excel 97-2003 binary format, with no conversion step and no Excel installation. A workbook still in that format has usually gone twenty years unexamined, which is where an unnoticed error has had longest to compound. Also .xlsx, .xlsm, .xltx and .xltm.
  • A command line for scheduled jobs, and a browser interface for everyone else.
  • Free and open source, Apache-2.0.

Try it

Download the latest release — an installer, or a single executable that needs nothing installed.

Builds are not yet code-signed, so Windows will tell you the publisher is unknown. That warning is accurate and you should treat any unsigned installer with the caution it deserves. Every release publishes a SHA-256 you can check before running it. Signing comes once there is a public release history to apply with.
